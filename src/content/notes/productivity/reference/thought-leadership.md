"Thought leadership" has a generic failure mode so common it's earned a reputation as an empty
phrase: a list of buzzwords, no attribution to a specific decision, and a conclusion that would be
true regardless of who wrote it. The useful version of the term is much narrower — content that
could only have been written by someone who actually made the decision, ran the incident, or has the
number.

---

## The generic failure mode

| Generic "thought leadership"                             | Practitioner-anchored                                                            |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "5 lessons every SRE should know"                        | "We cut SNOW ticket noise 60% — here's the one alert-routing change that did it" |
| No specific system, team, or outcome named               | Named decision, named trade-off, a number attached to it                         |
| Could be posted by anyone in the field                   | Could only be posted by someone who was in the room                              |
| Optimizes for engagement bait (hot takes, contrarianism) | Optimizes for being fact-checkable by a stranger                                 |

The tell is whether a reader could ask a specific follow-up question and get a specific answer. "5
lessons" posts collapse the moment someone asks "which system, though?"

## What earns the label

Content anchored to something concrete: a real incident post-mortem, a decision with a stated
trade-off, or a number that moved because of a specific action — _"onboarding went from 3 days to 30
minutes"_, _"80%+ reduction in AIOps noise"_ — each of those is one sentence, but each is
unfalsifiable-by-vagueness precisely because it's specific. Generic framing can't be fact-checked
because it never commits to a checkable claim in the first place.

## The cadence-vs-substance tension

A 2-posts/week cadence target and a "never post generic filler" standard pull against each other —
there won't always be a new incident or decision to anchor a post to on schedule. The honest
resolution isn't to lower the bar on off-weeks; it's to mine older, already-lived work (a past
incident, a decision made months ago whose outcome is now visible) rather than manufacture a take
that isn't grounded in anything.

## A test before publishing

- Does this reference an actual decision, incident, or number — not just a category of one?
- Could a stranger ask "which service?" or "what was the before/after?" and get a real answer?
- Would I say this exact thing, unhedged, in an interview follow-up?

If any answer is no, it's closer to generic framing than to earned thought leadership, regardless of
how confident the tone is.

## Where it depends on other skills

Thought leadership content is downstream of having gone through
[[critical-thinking|critical thinking]] about the underlying decision first — a post can only be
anchored and specific if the author actually interrogated _why_ the decision worked, not just _that_
it worked. Narrating it well is a separate, later step from having something real to narrate.
